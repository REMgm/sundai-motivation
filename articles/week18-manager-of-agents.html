Your AI Team Has More Members Than You Think: Why Every Executive Is Now a Manager of Agents.

Remco Vroom · Jun 27, 2026 · 8 min read

You started with one chatbot. Six months later, you have a research agent, a writing agent, a coding agent, a data agent, and something you don't have a name for yet that handles your calendar. You didn't notice the transition because it happened one tool at a time. But the org chart just changed.

Good morning, and welcome to SundAI Motivation.

Every Sunday I write one article for leaders who are serious about AI but don't have time to wade through the noise. No jargon. No hype. Just the signal you need to make better decisions this week.

A few weeks ago I wrote about becoming an AI practitioner -- sitting at the tiny desk, asking dumb questions, learning to use the tools your team uses. Two weeks ago, I wrote about why the brief is the bottleneck -- how the quality of what you ask determines the quality of what you get. This week is the natural next step: you're not just using one tool anymore. You're managing a system. And the skills that system demands are, surprisingly, the ones you already have.

This Week in AI

This Week in AI Multi-agent orchestration crossed from research into production this week. Anthropic's Claude now supports persistent project-scoped agents with shared memory. OpenAI shipped Codex as an asynchronous coding agent that works while you sleep. Google announced Gemini agent-to-agent protocols. The infrastructure for managing teams of AI agents -- not individual chatbots, but coordinated systems -- is landing in every enterprise platform simultaneously. The question is no longer "should we use AI?" It's "who manages the AI team?"

The Deep Dive: You Already Manage a Team You Haven't Named

The Simple Version

Most executives who have been using AI for six months are already managing multiple agents. They just haven't recognized it as management. The moment you do -- the moment you apply the same discipline to your AI team that you apply to your human team -- everything changes. Delegation gets sharper. Output gets better. And you stop wondering why "AI doesn't work for me" when the real problem is that you're managing six direct reports as if they were all the same person.

The Transition Nobody Noticed

Let me describe what happened to me over the last eight months, because I suspect something similar happened to you.

It started with ChatGPT. One tool. I used it for drafting, for brainstorming, for "help me think through this problem." It was a single relationship with a single tool. Easy to manage. No complexity.

Then I added Claude for longer strategic work -- it held more context, thought more carefully, pushed back on my assumptions. Now I had two. I started noticing I was choosing between them based on the task. That's a management decision, even though I didn't think of it that way.

Then Codex arrived -- an asynchronous coding agent that I could hand a task to before dinner and review the pull request in the morning. That's a third team member, and this one works overnight.

Then a research agent. Then a dedicated agent for monitoring my competitive landscape. Then one for scheduling, another for email triage, another for content production. Each one arrived as "just another tool." None of them felt like a hiring decision. But each one added a node to a system I was now responsible for coordinating.

I counted last week. I interact with eleven distinct AI agents in a typical workday. They have different strengths, different failure modes, different prompting styles, and different levels of autonomy. Some I trust to work unsupervised. Some I review line by line. Some I pair together on complex tasks. Some I've learned to never use for certain things because they consistently get it wrong.

That is not "using AI tools." That is management.

What Breaks at Scale

Here's what I learned the hard way -- and what I see breaking for every executive I coach who crosses the threshold from one or two agents to five or more:

At one agent, the problem is prompting. You're learning to communicate. You're figuring out how to ask the right question. This is the "tiny desk" phase I wrote about in Week 16. It's personal and manageable.

At three agents, the problem is context. Each agent has a different memory. You're repeating yourself. You're re-explaining your brand voice, your strategic priorities, your preferences. You feel like a new manager who has to onboard three people simultaneously but each one has amnesia every morning.

At five agents, the problem is delegation. You start sending the wrong task to the wrong agent. You use your writing agent for data work and wonder why the output is mediocre. You use your research agent for drafting and it produces something technically accurate but tonally dead. The agents aren't failing. You're mis-casting them. Just like a manager who assigns a financial analyst to write the press release.

At ten agents, the problem is orchestration. Tasks have dependencies. Agent A's output needs to feed into Agent B's input. Agent C needs to review what Agent D produced. You need escalation rules -- when does the agent decide, and when does it surface the decision to you? You need quality checkpoints. You need a workflow, not a series of individual conversations.

I hit each of these walls. Every single one. And the thing that got me through each one was not better technology. It was better management.

The Analogy

Think about the first time you managed a team of humans. You probably made the same mistakes everyone makes: you tried to do everything yourself, you gave vague briefs and blamed the output, you treated every team member the same instead of playing to their strengths, and you didn't have a system for reviewing work.

Then you learned. You learned to delegate clearly. You learned that the brief is the product -- if your brief is vague, the work will be vague. You learned that a great designer and a great analyst need fundamentally different management styles. You learned to build review checkpoints, escalation paths, and feedback loops.

Managing AI agents requires exactly the same skills. The team is silicon instead of carbon, but the management discipline is identical. The executives who are struggling with AI right now are not struggling because the technology is bad. They're struggling because they're treating a team of specialists as a single generalist. They're managing eleven agents the way they managed one chatbot, and they're getting exactly the results you'd expect from a manager who treats their entire team as interchangeable.

How I Manage My Agent Team

I'm going to share my actual system, because I think transparency is more useful than theory here.

Every agent has a role definition. Not a prompt. A role. My research agent has a defined scope: competitive intelligence, market signals, fact-checking claims. It knows what it's responsible for and what it's not. My strategic advisor agent has a different mandate: challenge my assumptions, rate its confidence, disagree with structure. These aren't casual instructions. They're job descriptions. And like any good job description, they took iteration to get right.

Every agent has a trust level. Some agents I trust to execute and deliver. I review their output but I don't supervise the process. Others I keep on a shorter leash -- I review intermediate steps, I check their reasoning, I ask for sources. This isn't about the agent being "good" or "bad." It's about the task's risk profile. Just like I manage a senior director differently from a new hire, I manage a well-tested agent workflow differently from an experimental one.

Agents hand off to each other. My research agent produces a briefing. That briefing becomes input for my strategic agent. The strategic agent produces a recommendation. That recommendation feeds into my writing agent for the client-facing version. Each handoff has a format. Each handoff has a quality gate. The chain doesn't run unsupervised -- I review at each transition. But the system produces in an hour what used to take a day.

I hold retrospectives. This sounds absurd, but it works. Every two weeks, I look at where my agent team failed. Where did I get output I had to throw away? Where did an agent confidently produce something wrong? Where did I send the right task to the wrong agent? Those failures are management failures, not technology failures. And they compound if you don't learn from them.

Your One Action This Week

This week, do an agent audit. Seriously. Here's how:

1. Count your agents. Open your phone and your laptop. Count every AI tool, assistant, chatbot, copilot, and agent you interact with in a typical week. Include the ones embedded in other products -- the AI in your email, the copilot in your IDE, the agent in your CRM. Most executives I coach discover they're already managing between five and fifteen agents. They just never counted.

2. Name the roles. Write down what each agent actually does for you. Not what it could do. What it does. If you can't articulate the role in one sentence, you're using it as a generalist when it should be a specialist. "I use Claude for thinking and ChatGPT for quick answers" is a start. "Claude is my strategic advisor for complex analysis; ChatGPT is my first-draft engine for routine communications" is a role definition.

3. Identify where you're mis-casting. Where are you sending tasks to agents that aren't suited for them? Where are you using your research agent for creative work, or your writing agent for data analysis? Every mis-cast task produces mediocre output and makes you think "AI isn't that useful" when the real problem is casting.

4. Build one handoff. Pick two agents. Design a workflow where Agent A's output becomes Agent B's input. Define the format. Run it once. You'll immediately see why orchestration matters -- and why treating agents as standalone tools leaves most of their value on the table.

The Pattern

Week 16: become a practitioner. Sit at the tiny desk. Learn the tools.

Week 18: the brief is the bottleneck. Learn to communicate with precision.

This week: you're managing a team. Learn to orchestrate.

The arc is the same arc every leader travels. Individual contributor. Communicator. Manager. The only difference is that this time, the team members run on GPUs instead of coffee.

The executives who treat each AI tool as a standalone app will hit a ceiling. The executives who recognize that they're managing a system -- and apply the management discipline they've spent their careers developing -- will discover that they have a team that never sleeps, never forgets a brief, and compounds its capability every single week.

You didn't hire these agents. They arrived one subscription at a time. But they're your team now. And they're waiting for a manager.
