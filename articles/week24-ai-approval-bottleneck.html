SundAI Motivation, Week 24

Your Fastest Agent Is Waiting for Approval

Remco Vroom · 2 August 2026 edition · 5 min read

Faster production creates value when the rest of the workflow can absorb it.

Your team can now produce a first draft before the meeting ends. The review meeting is still next Thursday.

That gap deserves attention. It is possible to improve a task dramatically while barely changing the time it takes to deliver something useful. The new capability has moved the pressure to another part of the process.

For many leaders, that is the next practical question in AI adoption: what happens immediately after the work gets faster?

Look at the queue

Consider an illustrative content team. Its old process generated five assets a week, and the reviewers could accept or reject five. AI increases production to twenty assets. Review capacity stays at five.

If all twenty are submitted, the queue grows by fifteen assets each week. That arithmetic is a scenario, not a benchmark. It shows why counting generated assets can give a misleading picture of operational progress.

The team is producing more material. The business is still releasing five accepted assets. Meanwhile, the reviewer has more to scan, more context to reconstruct, and more versions to keep apart.

A request to “generate another option” feels inexpensive at the moment of creation. It can carry a real downstream cost when somebody has to evaluate every option.

Measure work and waiting separately

Follow one item from the brief to its actual destination. Record when work happens and when the item waits. Include clarification, production, review, rework, approval, and release.

Human effort and elapsed time answer different questions. A reviewer may spend fifteen minutes on an item that has waited three days. A workflow can reduce effort without reducing that wait. It can also reduce elapsed time by coordinating work better without removing many human hours.

Both improvements can matter. Measure them separately so you can see which one you have achieved.

Also distinguish first drafts from accepted outputs. If ten generated options lead to one usable asset, the business has gained one asset and incurred the work of considering ten options. That may be an excellent trade. It still needs to be visible.

You do not need a company-wide process redesign to learn this. A few representative items, tracked honestly, can reveal where to investigate next.

Make review easier to perform

Start with the information arriving at the reviewer. A pile of drafts creates a discovery task. A recommendation with a compact decision record creates a more manageable review task.

For each item, show its purpose, the preferred option, what changed, the material claims requiring verification, and any unresolved exception. Provide the relevant evidence directly. Ask for a specific decision.

Keep the package proportional. A minor wording adjustment should not require the same review document as a new product promise. The aim is to reduce the time spent reconstructing context while preserving the judgment the decision needs.

You can also improve timing. If a customer commitment is the likely constraint, involve its owner before producing the full campaign around it. Discovering that the promise is unavailable after creating twenty assets turns a preventable dependency into rework.

This is a workflow-design recommendation: make the expensive uncertainties visible while they are still cheap to resolve.

Give routine work a clear route

Review every approval step and ask what decision it is meant to protect.

Some steps exist because ownership is unclear. Some repeat a check already completed elsewhere. Others protect a consequential promise, customer experience, or use of sensitive information. Those distinctions should shape the route.

For well-understood, low-impact work, the responsible owners may be able to agree on standard criteria and a simpler path. Work that changes claims, audience, commitments, or risk can take a more detailed route. Confirm these boundaries with the people accountable for the outcomes.

Treat a model's self-reported confidence cautiously. It should not be the sole reason a sensitive item bypasses review. Use observable characteristics of the work and demonstrated performance on relevant cases when deciding how much checking is appropriate.

Define what happens when the reviewer is unavailable, too. A named backup or an explicit pause is more useful than an approval request disappearing into a shared inbox.

Control what enters the queue

Sometimes the useful choice is to generate fewer things.

Agree on how many options the decision actually needs. Prioritize items against the business objective. Limit work in progress when additional drafts would simply wait. If you want a larger creative exploration, give it a separate selection stage before it reaches the final approver.

This preserves the value of inexpensive experimentation without making every experiment a demand on scarce review time.

Watch for an incentive problem as well. If producers are rewarded for output volume and reviewers are rewarded for catching every imperfection, the organization can build an ever-larger queue while both teams meet their individual targets.

Give them a shared measure of useful delivery.

Your one action this week

Track five recently completed items through your workflow. For each, separate active effort, waiting time, rework, and the final accepted result.

Find the longest recurring wait. Ask its owner what information, decision rule, timing change, or capacity adjustment would improve it. Make one change and observe the next five comparable items.

The next productivity gain may already be sitting in your organization, waiting for someone to make a decision. Follow the work far enough to find it.
