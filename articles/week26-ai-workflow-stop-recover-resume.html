Build an AI Fire Drill Before You Need One

Remco Vroom · 16 August 2026 edition · 5 min read

A short rehearsal reveals whether your team can stop, recover, and resume an agent workflow.

Ask the team running your most useful AI workflow a simple question: if it starts doing the wrong thing, who can stop it?

Then ask how you would know that it had actually stopped.

The second question is usually more revealing. A pause button on one screen may not describe the status of a queued action, a connected service, or work already handed to another system. Your technical owner should be able to explain what stopping means for the workflow you actually use.

A short rehearsal is a practical way to find out before the answer matters under pressure.

Choose a believable interruption

Consider an illustrative campaign workflow. An agent assembles material using approved product information, and a person reviews the package before release. During a rehearsal, the product owner withdraws one of the claims on which the draft depends.

What happens next?

The team should be able to identify the affected work, prevent further use of the withdrawn claim, locate the current source, and decide which outputs need another review. It should also know who can authorize the workflow to resume.

This is a useful exercise precisely because it is ordinary. You do not need an elaborate disaster scenario. A changed source, unavailable tool, duplicate request, or uncertain completion status can expose the mechanics that matter.

Choose an interruption that resembles something your operation could encounter. Rehearse in a test environment with synthetic or appropriately protected data, and without sending messages or changing real customer records.

Follow the recovery sequence

Begin with detection. What visible signal tells the team that something needs attention? Decide who receives that signal and what information helps them understand it. A vague “something failed” notification pushes diagnosis onto the recipient.

Next, establish how to contain the work. Can the responsible person pause new tasks, restrict the affected connection, or hold a particular output? Ask the technical owner to demonstrate the available controls and their limits.

Then inspect what already happened. Which steps completed? Which failed? Which are uncertain? Preserve enough of the activity record to establish the state without spreading sensitive information more widely than necessary.

Recovery follows from that state. The team may need to correct an input, restore an earlier version, repair a partial update, or finish a step manually. The right response depends on the workflow and the consequences of repeating or reversing an action.

Finally, make restarting a deliberate decision. Confirm that the problem is addressed and that the remaining work still has the right inputs, permissions, and owner.

Repeating can be a decision

“Try again” is an attractive response to an uncertain result. It is not always a neutral one.

In an illustrative record-creation workflow, a lost confirmation could leave the team unsure whether the first record exists. Repeating the request without checking could create a duplicate. Your implementation needs a way to resolve that uncertainty safely.

Ask your technical owner how the system distinguishes an action that failed from one that succeeded without a confirmation. Ask which operations can be repeated safely and which require an outcome check first.

You do not need to prescribe the engineering mechanism yourself. You do need an operational answer that your team can demonstrate.

Anthropic's foundational agent guidance recommends testing in controlled environments and using appropriate boundaries and stopping conditions. A rehearsal applies that principle to the people and systems around a specific business workflow. Building effective agents, December 2024.

Make the fallback usable

A fallback that exists only in a document may not help the person dealing with the interruption.

Confirm that someone has access to the necessary source material, knows the manual route, and can locate the last accepted output. If the fallback depends on a single specialist, include their absence in a later rehearsal.

Keep the fallback proportional to the service. An internal research task can often wait. A customer-facing process may need a more immediate alternative. Let the accountable business owner define the required continuity, then test whether the arrangement provides it.

Measure the exercise in terms people can act on: time to detect the issue, time to establish the state, affected outputs located, and unresolved dependencies. These observations tell you what to improve. They should not become a performance contest that discourages people from revealing uncertainty.

Your one action this week

Book a short session with the business owner, the person operating the workflow, and its technical owner. Choose one realistic interruption and rehearse detection, containment, inspection, recovery, and restart.

Keep a one-page record of who acts, where the controls are, what evidence is needed, and what you discovered. Fix the most consequential gap and repeat that part of the exercise.

Do not reward a flawless presentation. Reward the moment someone says, “We do not yet know whether that action completed.” That admission gives you a concrete uncertainty to resolve while the stakes are controlled.

The practical benefit is confidence based on a demonstrated recovery path. Your team can use the capability with a clearer understanding of what to do when the ordinary working day becomes less ordinary.
