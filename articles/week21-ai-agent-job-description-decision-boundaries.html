Your AI Agent Needs a Job Description

Remco Vroom · 12 July 2026 edition · 5 min read

The next step after a better brief is a clear boundary around what your agent can decide.

Give an agent the task of preparing a customer proposal and you have given it a goal. You have not necessarily told it whether it can change the price, promise a delivery date, contact the customer, or book the people needed to deliver the work.

Those are four different decisions. A polished proposal can hide all four.

This is where an AI experiment starts becoming an operating responsibility. The question becomes: which decisions have we actually delegated?

In the previous edition, we explored the missing knowledge inside a brief. The next step is to examine the authority inside it. An agent can have useful context and still be the wrong place for a particular decision.

The job is bigger than the task

Imagine an illustrative sales workflow. A team uses AI to assemble a proposal from approved service descriptions, a customer brief, and previous work. The draft is persuasive. It also includes a delivery commitment that nobody in the delivery team has accepted.

The sentence looks like copy. To the customer, it is a promise.

That distinction matters more than how quickly the draft arrived. The agent's task was to prepare a recommendation. The workflow allowed that recommendation to look like an authorized commitment.

You can address this before buying another tool. Give the workflow a short job description covering its purpose, inputs, decisions, limits, and accountable owner. Then make the actual system respect that description.

A sentence in a prompt is useful guidance. It is not a substitute for restricting the tools and permissions available to the agent. If publishing requires approval, the agent should not have an unrestricted publishing route that bypasses it.

Use three decision categories

Start with work the agent can complete within an agreed boundary. In our proposal example, it might summarize supplied requirements, assemble approved material, and identify unanswered questions. These activities have a clear scope and a reviewable output.

Next, identify decisions it can recommend. It might suggest a delivery approach or flag that a commercial exception could help. The recommendation should carry its assumptions and evidence. A named person decides whether to accept it.

Finally, identify actions that need explicit authorization: sending the proposal, changing a price, allocating people, or making a promise on the organization's behalf. These are illustrative boundaries; your actual categories should reflect the consequences of your workflow.

The important detail is the transition between categories. A recommendation does not become authorized because it survived into the final paragraph. An agent must not treat silence, a missing field, or an unavailable reviewer as approval.

For a low-impact internal draft, it can often continue and mark an assumption. For a commitment outside its authority, it should stop at the boundary and return the decision needed.

Give the boundary an owner

“Human oversight” sounds reassuring until the human is a distribution list.

Name the person who can accept the work and the backup who can act when that person is unavailable. Define what the reviewer receives: the proposed action, supporting evidence, relevant changes, and unresolved questions.

Otherwise you have transferred the work of discovering the risk to the busiest person in the process.

The same principle applies to stopping. Decide what happens if the input is incomplete, two sources conflict, the cost limit is reached, or a tool reports an uncertain result. The response should fit the situation. Asking one focused question may be enough. In other cases, the workflow needs to pause and preserve its progress.

Anthropic's foundational guidance on agents describes the value of environmental feedback, human checkpoints, and stopping conditions. The management implication is to decide where those mechanisms belong before granting a workflow more freedom. Building effective agents, December 2024.

Test the uncomfortable cases

Do a short rehearsal using cases that challenge the boundary.

Give the proposal workflow an outdated price sheet. Ask it to include a delivery date without confirmed capacity. Make the designated reviewer unavailable. Check what it actually does.

Does it explain the missing decision? Does it preserve a useful draft? Does it route the exception to the right owner? Can it accidentally send the work anyway?

A successful demonstration of the happy path tells you that the system can perform a task. These rehearsals tell you whether the surrounding organization has decided how to operate it.

Avoid making the boundary so restrictive that every formatting choice requires a meeting. The point is to make ordinary work easier to complete while keeping consequential decisions visible. Review repeated escalations and ask whether they reveal a missing rule, insufficient context, or a genuine need for human judgment.

Your one action this week

Choose one agent workflow already used by your team. Complete this short delegation note:

  • Its purpose and accepted output.
  • The information and tools it may use.
  • The decisions it may complete and those it may only recommend.
  • The actions requiring authorization and the person who can give it.
  • The conditions that cause it to ask, pause, or stop.

Then ask your technical owner to demonstrate that the permissions and workflow match the note. Test one ordinary task and one boundary case together.

You will probably discover that some decisions were delegated deliberately and others simply slipped into the process. That is useful progress. You now have something concrete to fix.

An agent becomes easier to trust when everyone can see the work it owns, the decisions it can make, and the point at which somebody else takes responsibility.

That is a job description worth writing.
