The Handoff Is Where AI Work Breaks

Remco Vroom · 9 August 2026 edition · 5 min read

Specialist agents need a shared understanding of the work they pass between them.

The research is careful. The writing is persuasive. The presentation looks excellent. The recommendation is wrong because a qualification disappeared between the research and the draft.

No individual output looks obviously broken. The failure lives in the transition.

As you divide work among people and agents, that transition deserves as much attention as the specialists themselves. A good handoff tells the next participant what has been established, what remains uncertain, and what they are expected to do with the result.

A relay needs more than fast runners

Imagine an illustrative market-entry project. One agent researches customer demand. Another examines delivery requirements. A third prepares a commercial recommendation.

The demand research concerns large enterprises in one country. The delivery analysis assumes smaller customers across an entire region. The commercial draft combines both as if they describe the same opportunity.

Each specialist has completed a plausible assignment. The combined recommendation rests on incompatible definitions.

The team does not need more eloquent summaries. It needs an agreed frame: which customer, which market, which period, and which decision.

Write that frame once and make it part of every assignment. If a specialist discovers a reason to change it, the change should be visible to the person coordinating the work and to anyone whose conclusions depend on it.

Otherwise the workflow can quietly become several different projects wearing the same title.

Pass a decision record with the output

For a substantive handoff, ask for a compact record alongside the work. It should identify the task completed, the sources or inputs used, the material assumptions, unresolved questions, and the next decision required.

Separate evidence from interpretation. “Three interviewees mentioned this problem” is an observation in our hypothetical project. “This problem will support a new business line” is a conclusion that needs further support. A shorter summary should preserve that difference.

Keep references to the underlying work. The next participant should be able to inspect an important qualification without repeating the whole research exercise. A link to an approved source is often more useful than another confident paraphrase of it.

Anthropic's account of building a multi-agent research system describes how unclear delegation led to duplicated work and gaps. Its experience supports giving specialists clear objectives, output expectations, and boundaries. The same care should extend to the work they exchange. Make receiving an active step

A handoff is complete when the recipient can use the work. That requires a small acceptance check.

Does the output answer the assigned question? Does it use the agreed definitions? Can the evidence be inspected? Are the assumptions acceptable for the next task? Is there an unresolved dependency that would make continuing premature?

These checks can be brief. Their value comes from being explicit. If the next participant cannot proceed, return a precise explanation of what is missing instead of producing a polished approximation.

For the market-entry example, the commercial agent should flag the inconsistent customer segments before combining the analyses. The project owner can then choose a shared segment or commission the missing work.

That is a useful interruption. It prevents a mismatch from acquiring the appearance of certainty as it moves through increasingly polished deliverables.

Give integration one owner

Specialists optimize for their assignments. Someone still needs to accept the overall result.

Name the person responsible for resolving contradictory findings, deciding whether more research is worthwhile, and confirming that the final recommendation answers the original business question. An orchestration tool can coordinate activity, but organizational accountability still needs a recognizable home.

When an upstream input changes, identify the downstream work affected by it. A revised customer definition may invalidate both the commercial model and the presentation. Simply rerunning the last writing step can preserve conclusions that no longer fit.

Keep the accepted version of important inputs visible. The team should know which source a recommendation relies on and which outputs need reconsideration after a change.

Split work only where the split helps

More specialists create more handoffs to manage. If two tasks constantly exchange the same context or depend on decisions being made together, separating them may add coordination work without improving the result.

Start with a small arrangement that fits the problem. Parallel research can be useful when questions are sufficiently independent. A tightly connected negotiation or evolving recommendation may benefit from fewer transitions and a more continuous working context.

Judge the arrangement by the quality and cost of the accepted outcome, including integration and rework. Counting agents tells you very little about whether the system is useful.

Your one action this week

Choose one recurring handoff between an agent and a person, or between two agents. Look at the last output that crossed it and ask the recipient what they had to reconstruct.

Turn those missing pieces into a short handoff requirement. Test it on the next assignment, including one case with incomplete evidence or conflicting definitions.

Then remove anything in the requirement that adds bulk without helping the recipient act. You are designing a useful exchange, not an administrative attachment.

A well-run AI workflow preserves meaning as work moves. The quality of the final answer depends on what survives the journey.
