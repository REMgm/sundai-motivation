SundAI Motivation, Week 23

Your AI Is Remembering the Wrong Things

Remco Vroom · 26 July 2026 edition · 5 min read

Shared memory becomes valuable when it carries an owner, a source, and a reason to remain current.

An old price in a new proposal is easy to mistake for an AI reasoning failure. Sometimes the reasoning is perfectly consistent with the information supplied. The information is simply out of date.

That is an uncomfortable possibility for any business building a shared knowledge base. Making more company knowledge available can help an agent. It also gives the agent more opportunities to retrieve yesterday's answer to today's question.

The leadership task is to decide which knowledge deserves to guide current work and who keeps it fit for that purpose.

Storage does not establish authority

Imagine an illustrative marketing team preparing a launch. Its shared folder contains an early positioning deck, a revised product description, an approved claims document, and notes from a brainstorming session.

All four mention the same product. Only one establishes which claims are approved. The newest file could still be an unapproved idea. The longest document could be the least relevant. A persuasive summary could remove the caveat that made an earlier statement acceptable.

The folder contains information. The workflow needs a way to interpret the status of that information.

Start by identifying the authoritative source for the decisions that matter. If the approved claims document governs product promises, make that relationship explicit. If a local market can vary the wording, record who authorizes the variation and where the accepted version lives.

An agent should not have to infer organizational authority from whichever paragraph happens to resemble its question most closely.

Give memory a maintenance contract

For reusable knowledge, attach a few simple facts: where it came from, who owns it, when it was last checked, and when it needs another review. Add the scope in which it applies.

Scope is easy to miss. A statement can be current and accurate for one market, product, or customer while being inappropriate elsewhere. A negotiated exception should not silently become a general commercial rule.

Different information needs different review triggers. A durable brand principle may be reviewed infrequently. A product capability needs a check when the product changes. A staffing assumption may need confirmation before every proposal that depends on it.

An expiry or review date should trigger revalidation. It should not automatically erase records that the organization needs to retain. Distinguish the archive, which preserves history, from the approved knowledge that guides current decisions.

That gives people a practical way to keep useful memory without making every past statement an instruction for the future.

Curate what the task actually needs

The temptation is to solve every disappointing answer by supplying another folder. Before doing that, ask whether the agent received the right material in a usable form.

Anthropic's September 2025 context-engineering guidance emphasizes selecting relevant information for a task and explains how excessive or poorly curated context can reduce usefulness. More available material does not remove the need to choose carefully. Effective context engineering for AI agents.

For the launch example, a compact brief with the current product description, approved claims, audience, and explicit unresolved questions may be more useful than the entire history of the launch. Keep links back to the underlying sources so a reviewer can inspect the detail when needed.

This is a recommendation about organization, not a promise that one document layout fixes every model. Test the arrangement against the work your team actually performs.

Access also needs to follow the task. Shared memory does not mean every agent or colleague should receive every document. Preserve the permissions of the underlying information and have the technical owner demonstrate how retrieval respects them.

Treat corrections as candidates

When someone edits an AI draft, the correction may contain a valuable lesson. It may also reflect a one-off preference, a temporary exception, or an error by the reviewer.

Capture the proposed lesson with its context. Then decide whether it belongs in shared knowledge, in a particular customer's instructions, or only in the current task. A named owner should approve material changes to information that other workflows will rely on.

Otherwise a system designed to learn can turn a local exception into an organization-wide habit.

Make contradictions visible. If two apparently authoritative sources disagree, the useful response is to identify the disagreement and route it for resolution. Quietly blending them into a confident answer hides the very issue the organization needs to settle.

Your one action this week

Choose five facts your agents reuse often. They might be a product promise, a pricing rule, a brand instruction, a delivery assumption, and a customer preference.

For each, identify its source, owner, scope, last verification, and next review trigger. Then ask the workflow to answer a question that depends on it. Check whether it finds the right version and preserves the relevant caveat.

If a fact has no owner, assign one. If several versions compete, establish which one governs. If the workflow cannot distinguish an approved rule from an old draft, fix that before adding more material.

Useful memory is maintained knowledge. The advantage comes from knowing which part of the past should influence the next decision, and making that judgment available to everyone doing the work.
