SundAI Motivation, Week 27

Give Your Brand a Test Suite

Remco Vroom · 23 August 2026 edition · 5 min read

Turn the judgments behind good work into examples your team can use to evaluate AI.

Your brand guidelines say the company is confident, warm, and human.

So does almost everyone else's.

Those words can be useful starting points. They do not tell an agent how your brand should respond to a frustrated customer, qualify a product claim, or explain a limitation without sounding evasive.

The difference becomes clearer in examples. Show the work you would accept, the work you would reject, and the reason for the distinction. That gives your team something concrete to evaluate when a prompt, model, or workflow changes.

Test the promise behind the style

Imagine an illustrative service brand that promises clarity. Its AI-generated email is friendly, concise, and grammatically perfect. It also avoids the customer's actual question about what happens next.

The style passes. The promise fails.

A useful brand evaluation should examine both. Does the output look and sound appropriate? Does it behave in a way that supports what the brand claims to offer?

For that service brand, clarity might mean stating the next step, identifying the responsible party, and acknowledging what is still unknown. The same standard could apply across an email, a support response, and a website explanation without forcing identical wording.

This is where the brand team can contribute something more durable than a collection of adjectives: a set of judgments that other people can understand and apply.

Start with situations that matter

Choose a small collection of real, appropriately anonymized situations. Include a routine request, an incomplete brief, a dissatisfied customer, a tempting but unsupported claim, and a case where the organization needs to explain a limitation.

Add examples that distinguish your brand from a competent generic response. If two versions are both accurate, why would you prefer one? Is it more useful, more direct, more respectful of the reader's time, or more consistent with the experience you want to provide?

Write down the reason. “This feels more like us” may express a valid judgment, but it leaves the next reviewer guessing. Explain the observable choice behind that feeling.

Use a separate set of examples to guide creation and to evaluate the result. Reusing the same examples for both makes it harder to tell whether the workflow can handle a fresh situation.

Separate essentials from preferences

Some criteria should be firm. An invented product capability, a fabricated quotation, or an unsupported customer promise should not be rescued by excellent tone.

Other criteria allow a range of good answers. There may be several effective openings, several suitable visual treatments, and more than one way to express warmth. Preserve that room for creative judgment.

This distinction prevents the evaluation from turning into a machine for producing sameness. You are protecting the important standards while allowing the work to remain alive.

Anthropic's January 2026 evaluation guide describes combining automated checks, model-based assessment, and human judgment, including the need to calibrate model-based graders with people. That supports a layered approach rather than treating one generated score as final authority. Demystifying evals for AI agents.

Check the whole experience

Brand evaluation should cover the material a customer actually encounters. An accurate paragraph can still sit beside an image that implies something untrue. A responsible headline can lead to a page that fails to deliver its promise.

For generated imagery, ask whether the picture communicates the idea honestly, contains unintended text or visual defects, and implies a real person, customer, or event that did not occur. A conceptual illustration can be striking while remaining clearly appropriate to the story.

For an article, compare the headline with the evidence and the conclusion. For a campaign, examine the relationship between the advertisement, landing page, and offer. The customer experiences the sequence, even when separate teams or agents produced its parts.

Test useful combinations of text and image. Approving each asset in isolation can miss a misleading impression created by the two together.

Use changes as a reason to recheck

When the team adopts a new model or revises a prompt, run the evaluation examples again. Compare the important outcomes with the previous version and inspect meaningful differences.

Look for improvements as well as regressions. A new version may handle difficult explanations better while becoming less precise about product qualifications. That is a decision for the owners of those standards to understand before broadening use.

Keep collecting failures from real work. Add representative examples after resolving them, and remove duplicates that no longer add useful coverage. A small maintained set can be more usable than a large neglected one.

Agree who owns the evaluation itself. Product facts, brand judgment, and local-market requirements may belong to different people. Make their responsibilities explicit so disputed results lead to a decision instead of another round of vague feedback.

Your one action this week

Bring five accepted and five rejected pieces of work to a short session with the people who review your brand's output. Remove confidential details and identify the reason behind each decision.

Turn those reasons into a compact evaluation guide. Choose a fresh brief, generate an output, and have two reviewers apply the guide independently. Discuss where they disagree and improve the criteria.

You will learn something about the AI workflow. You may learn just as much about the decisions your organization has left implicit.

A recognizable brand depends on repeated choices. Make the important choices visible enough that your people and your tools can carry them into the next piece of work.
