Your AI Pilot Needs an Exit Date

Remco Vroom · 30 August 2026 edition · 5 min read

A useful experiment ends with a decision to expand, repair, or stop.

The demo is still impressive. The pilot is still promising. Another month has passed without anyone deciding what happens next.

An AI pilot can become a comfortable place to keep an idea alive. It attracts enthusiasm, produces occasional examples, and avoids the obligations of a service that people depend on. The organization learns something, but the decision keeps moving.

Give the pilot an exit date and a clear question to answer. That makes the learning useful to the person deciding where the business should invest its attention next.

State the decision before the experiment

Consider an illustrative proposal-support pilot. A team wants to know whether an agent can help produce accepted proposal drafts with less effort while preserving accuracy and appropriate commercial review.

That is a testable question. “Explore the potential of AI in sales” is much harder to conclude.

Define the workflow, its boundaries, the people using it, and the result that would justify further investment. Identify the current process you will compare it with. Include the quality standard and the work required to check the output.

Choose a review date that fits the volume and variation of the task. A fortnight may produce plenty of evidence for a daily workflow and almost none for an infrequent specialist assignment. The calendar should help you collect enough relevant examples to make a decision.

Agree what you will do if the evidence is insufficient. A limited extension can be reasonable when it has a specific unanswered question and a credible way to answer it.

Count the support around the demonstration

During a pilot, enthusiastic people often supply invisible assistance. They repair inputs, answer questions, select the best examples, and quietly correct weak outputs before anyone else sees them.

Record that effort. It may be a sensible investment in learning, but it affects what the organization would need to operate the workflow at a larger scale.

For the proposal example, measure the time spent preparing inputs, producing the draft, reviewing commitments, and correcting errors. Include tool and integration costs where they matter. Examine the accepted output, not just the first generated version.

Keep the pilot's task mix visible. If participants choose only the easiest proposals, the results may tell you something useful about those proposals. They provide weaker evidence for every proposal the organization handles.

Compare like with like where possible, and state where the comparison remains imperfect. A decision can be useful without pretending the experiment is more precise than it is.

Make three outcomes legitimate

Expansion is appropriate when the evidence supports a defined next scope and someone accepts responsibility for operating it. Expand in a bounded step so you can observe whether the result holds with more users, different inputs, or greater volume.

Repair is appropriate when the pilot exposes a specific solvable problem. Perhaps source information is inconsistent, review is too slow, or the task was divided poorly. Name the repair, its owner, and the evidence that would show it worked. Give the repair its own decision date.

Stopping is appropriate when the workflow fails the required standard, costs too much to operate, or addresses a problem that is no longer important enough. Preserve the useful learning and release the people involved to do something else.

Avoid making “continue exploring” the default fourth outcome. If exploration remains worthwhile, say what uncertainty you are exploring and why resolving it is worth the additional effort.

Choose the simplest adequate arrangement

A pilot can succeed by showing that a smaller solution is enough. A clearer source document, a template, or a limited assistant may address the problem without a complex agent system.

Anthropic's foundational guidance recommends beginning with simple approaches and adding complexity when it improves outcomes. Applied to a pilot, that means testing whether the additional coordination and autonomy earn their place. Building effective agents, December 2024.

The business objective should survive a change in technical approach. If the goal is faster, more accurate proposal preparation, the pilot can be valuable even when the eventual solution looks different from the original demonstration.

That flexibility is easier when the sponsor is attached to the outcome rather than to a particular architecture.

Name the owner after the pilot

Before expansion, establish who owns the source information, quality checks, costs, user support, and changes to the workflow. Decide who can pause it and what fallback is available.

These responsibilities can sit with existing teams. They still need to be accepted. Otherwise the successful experiment becomes an unofficial service maintained by the person who happened to build it.

Ask what ordinary operation looks like on a week when the original champion is away. The answer will reveal dependencies that a good demonstration can conceal.

Your one action this week

Take one active AI pilot and write a one-page decision brief: the question being tested, comparison process, accepted quality standard, full operating effort, evidence gathered, unresolved uncertainty, decision owner, and review date.

At that review, choose expansion, a specific repair, or closure. If you extend the experiment, record exactly what the extra time is expected to establish.

A pilot earns its place by helping the organization make a better decision. Give it a clear route to that decision, and give the people involved permission to learn something other than what they hoped to prove.
