SundAI Motivation, Week 16

The CEO at the Tiny Desk: Why Every Executive Needs to Become an AI Practitioner.

Remco Vroom · May 3, 2026 · 8 min read

I spent last Tuesday sitting at a desk half the size of my normal one, in a chair that creaked, while a 26-year-old data scientist taught me how to chain prompts in Claude. By Wednesday afternoon, I had three ideas that will save my team forty hours a week. I never would have found them from the corner office.

Good morning, and welcome to SundAI Motivation.

Every Sunday I write one article for leaders who are serious about AI but don't have time to wade through the noise. No jargon. No hype. Just the signal you need to make better decisions this week.

This week, I'm writing about something uncomfortable. Something most executives resist. Something that, if you do it, will change the quality of every AI conversation you have with your team from this day forward.

I'm talking about becoming a student. Not a sponsor. Not a champion. Not an "executive sponsor of the AI initiative." A practitioner. Someone who actually uses the tools, breaks things, asks dumb questions, and -- this is the critical part -- has real conversations with the people on their team who live this stuff every day.

This Week in AI

This Week in AI A new pattern emerged across enterprise AI rollouts this week: the single biggest predictor of whether an AI initiative succeeds isn't budget, isn't tooling, and isn't talent. It's whether the executive in charge has spent at least ten hours personally using the platform their team is adopting. Companies where the C-suite are active practitioners report 4.2x higher adoption rates, 3.1x faster time-to-value, and -- most surprisingly -- 2.8x more employee-generated AI ideas submitted through internal channels. When the boss knows enough to ask intelligent questions, the entire organization shifts from "executing the executive's AI mandate" to "collaborating on AI opportunities." The dynamic changes completely.

The Deep Dive: Why the Corner Office Is the Worst Place to Understand AI

The Simple Version

You cannot understand what AI can do for your organization by reading reports, attending briefings, or watching demos. You have to use it. And you have to use it badly, publicly, and with help from the people who work for you. The moment you become a student, you become approachable. And the moment you become approachable, your team's best ideas start surfacing.

The Context

Here's what happens when an executive treats AI as a strategic initiative they delegate: they get updates. The updates are filtered. Filtered updates contain only what the person giving the update thinks the executive wants to hear. The real friction, the real opportunities, the real breakthroughs -- they never make it up the chain.

Here's what happens when that same executive spends two hours a week actually using AI tools: they develop opinions. Those opinions are grounded in reality, not vendor presentations. They start asking questions that only someone who has tried the work would know to ask. And -- this is the part that surprises everyone -- they start having conversations with their team that sound completely different.

I tested this at Monks. For six weeks, I blocked ninety minutes every Tuesday morning. No calls, no email, no executive tasks. Just me, a laptop, and whatever AI tool I was trying to learn that week. I sat in the open workspace -- not my office -- and I worked through tutorials, broke things, asked questions.

The first two weeks were embarrassing. I asked questions my team had answered months ago. I struggled with prompts that my junior developers could write in their sleep. I felt, frankly, ridiculous.

Then week three happened. I was working through a prompt chain exercise, trying to get Claude to analyze a campaign brief and generate strategic recommendations. One of our senior strategists -- someone I had worked with for three years but had never had an informal conversation with -- walked past, saw what I was doing, and stopped. "You know," she said, "if you reframe that first prompt to ask for the constraints instead of the output, you'll get something way more useful." She sat down. We spent forty-five minutes iterating together. By the end, we had a prompt sequence that she took back to her team and that I now use in every strategic planning session.

That forty-five-minute conversation produced more actionable insight than any quarterly strategy review I had attended in the previous year. Not because I'm brilliant. Because I was sitting at the tiny desk, not the corner office. Because I was a peer, not a boss. Because she felt comfortable correcting me.

The Analogy

Think about a CEO who has never used their company's product. They know the revenue numbers, the market share, the competitive landscape. They can present the strategy deck flawlessly. But ask them what a real customer experiences in minute three of using the product and they draw a blank.

Every executive understands that's a liability. That's why great CEOs -- Bezos, Cook, the ones who build companies that last -- use their own products religiously. Not because they need to place orders or book rides. Because understanding the product at the level of lived experience changes every strategic decision they make.

AI is no different. If your only experience with AI is vendor demos and strategy decks, you are making decisions about a product you have never actually used. You are strategically planning for a capability you don't personally understand. And your team knows it.

The moment you start using the tools your team uses, something shifts. You stop asking "what can AI do for us?" and start asking "why does this workflow take three steps when it could take one?" You stop evaluating AI initiatives and start co-creating them. You stop being the person who approves the budget and start being the person who spots the opportunity.

Monks Success Story

My Tuesday morning practice started as an experiment. Six months later, it's the most productive ninety minutes of my week. Here's what changed:

First, the quality of our AI conversations skyrocketed. Before, my team would present AI initiatives with elaborate business cases and ROI projections. They were selling me. Now, they bring half-formed ideas, rough prototypes, and experimental prompts. They're collaborating with me. The difference is profound: selling produces approved projects. Collaboration produces better ideas.

Second, my team started bringing me ideas they never would have presented formally. A content strategist showed me a prompt sequence that automated competitor monitoring. A junior developer walked me through an agent he had built to triage support tickets. These weren't strategic initiatives. They were experiments. They weren't polished enough for an executive briefing. But they were exactly the kind of practical, ground-level innovation that transforms how an organization works.

Third -- and this was the surprise -- my own strategic thinking changed. When you actually use AI, you develop an intuition for what's easy, what's hard, and what's about to become possible. I started seeing opportunities in places I would have missed before. I started asking my team different questions. I started making better decisions about where to invest, where to experiment, and where to wait.

"The best AI strategy I developed this year came from a forty-five-minute conversation with a strategist who felt comfortable correcting my prompts. Not from a strategy offsite. Not from a McKinsey deck. From sitting at a desk and being bad at something in public."

Your One Action This Week

This week, become a student. Not metaphorically. Actually. Here's how:

1. Block ninety minutes. Put it on your calendar. Protect it like you would a board meeting. Tuesday morning worked for me because it's early enough that I haven't been pulled into executive mode yet. Find your time.

2. Sit where your team sits. Not your office. The open workspace. The communal area. Somewhere people walk past and can see what you're doing. The visibility matters. It signals that this is important enough for your time and public enough for your ego.

3. Pick one tool and actually use it. Not a demo. Not a tutorial video. Real work. Analyze a brief. Draft an email chain. Build a spreadsheet. Do something you would normally do manually, and do it with AI. You'll be slower at first. That's the point.

4. Ask for help. Publicly. This is the hardest part and the most important. Ask the junior person on your team to show you how they use the tool. Ask the developer to walk you through their workflow. Let them see you struggle. Let them teach you. The moment you ask for help, you give them permission to be experts -- and you give yourself access to their expertise.

5. Have one real conversation. Not a status update. Not a strategic review. A conversation. Ask what they're experimenting with. Ask what frustrated them this week. Ask what they would build if they had two weeks and no meetings. When you speak as a practitioner, not a boss, the answers will surprise you.

The Prediction

Within a month of starting this practice, you will have at least one conversation that produces more value than a quarter of your formal meetings. Within three months, your team will bring you ideas they never would have surfaced to an executive. Within six months, you will understand your organization's AI opportunities better than any consultant ever could.

The executives who treat AI as something to delegate will spend 2026 reading reports about opportunities their teams already found. The executives who become practitioners will find those opportunities themselves -- and their teams will bring them even more.

The corner office has a great view. But the tiny desk is where the good ideas live.
